# Playbook: Affiliate Marketing

Applies to promoting third-party products/brands for commission — single-brand or, more
commonly, an audience being offered multiple different brands over time. Assumes
`core-principles.md` and `the-fundamentals.md` are already loaded.

## How the funnel changes when you don't own the product

The funnel in `the-fundamentals.md` still applies, with one structural difference: **you don't
control the Close stage.** The vendor owns the checkout page, the price, the guarantee, and the
fulfillment. That changes where your leverage actually is:

- Stages 1–4 (Awareness → Lead Magnet → Nurture → Offer) are where almost all of your
  leverage lives, since they're the only parts of the funnel you fully control.
- A weak vendor checkout will cap your conversion no matter how good your copy is — which is
  why offer selection (below) matters as much as the copy itself.
- "Retain/Refer" shifts meaning: you're not retaining a customer of *your* product, you're
  retaining a subscriber's trust in *you* so they'll buy through your link again on the next
  promotion.

## Offer selection: what to vet before promoting anything

Don't promote a product just because it pays well. Check, in this order:

1. **Fit** — does this solve a problem your specific audience actually has? A high-payout
   offer that's a poor fit converts worse and costs you trust for the next promotion.
2. **Vendor reputation** — refund rate, support quality, and how long they've been paying
   affiliates on time. A great product with a slow/unreliable payout program isn't worth the
   relationship risk.
3. **Commission structure** — one-time vs. recurring (recurring compounds — prioritize it
   when the product fits), payout percentage, and cookie duration (see Metrics below).
4. **Compliance** — confirm the vendor allows the channels you plan to use (some prohibit
   paid ads on their branded terms, email to purchased lists, etc.) before you invest time
   building assets around it.
5. **Refund/churn rate**, if available — a high refund rate usually means the sales page
   over-promises relative to the product, which will surface as buyer's remorse and hurt your
   credibility with that segment of your list.

Reject offers that fail (1) even if they're strong on (2)–(4) — audience fit is the one thing
that protects the long-term relationship.

## Finding programs and networks

Offers generally come from two sources, and they differ enough to affect which offers you can
realistically access and how you get paid:

- **Affiliate networks** (e.g. ShareASale, CJ, Impact, Awin, Amazon Associates) — a single
  application gives access to many vendors' programs, with standardized (if sometimes
  delayed) reporting and payout. Easiest starting point, but commission rates are often lower
  than a direct relationship and payout terms are set by the network, not negotiable per-deal.
- **Direct vendor/in-house programs** — apply directly to a specific brand's own affiliate
  program. Often better commission rates and sometimes custom deals once you have a track
  record, but you're dependent on that vendor's own tracking/payment reliability with no
  network intermediary to fall back on if something goes wrong.

When starting out, networks are the faster path to your first few offers; move higher-volume
promotions to direct relationships once a program is proven to convert and you have leverage
(traffic/results) to negotiate better terms.

## Link infrastructure and tracking

Bare affiliate links are fragile and hard to manage at any scale — set this up before you're
promoting more than one or two offers:

- **Use cloaked/redirect links** (e.g. `yoursite.com/go/product-name` redirecting to the real
  affiliate URL) instead of pasting raw affiliate URLs into content and emails. This makes
  links shorter and more trustworthy-looking, and — critically — lets you update the
  destination in one place if a vendor changes their URL structure, rather than editing every
  piece of content that ever linked to them.
- **Tag with UTM parameters** (or your link tool's equivalent) so traffic sources are visible
  in your own analytics, not just the vendor's — you want to know which piece of content or
  email actually drove a click, not just that the vendor credited a sale to you.
- **Check links on a schedule.** Vendors restructure URLs, discontinue products, or shut down
  programs without notice; a broken affiliate link is a dead end for a reader and lost revenue
  for you. Periodically audit older content's links, especially anything ranking well in
  search (see `playbooks/seo-content.md`) since that content keeps earning long after
  publication.
- **Keep redirect links on a domain you control**, not a third-party shortener you don't own —
  if the shortener disappears or gets flagged/blocked by a platform, every link you've ever
  shared through it breaks at once.

## Platform and income risk

Affiliate income is unusually concentrated in a small number of dependencies outside your
control — plan for that instead of being surprised by it:

- **A single traffic source failing can zero out income overnight** — a search algorithm
  update that drops your rankings, a social platform ban, or an ad account suspension. Don't
  build the entire business on one channel; use paid, organic/SEO, and list-based promotion
  (see `playbooks/paid-ads.md`, `playbooks/seo-content.md`, `playbooks/email-marketing.md`) as
  genuinely separate legs, not just theoretically diversified.
- **A single vendor/program ending or changing terms can zero out that revenue line** — this
  is the direct argument for the multi-brand approach in this playbook: your own list is the
  asset that survives any one program change, since you can promote a replacement offer to the
  same audience.
- **Track revenue by channel and by vendor separately** (see `playbooks/analytics.md`), and
  treat any channel or vendor providing a large majority of income as a flagged risk to
  actively diversify away from, even while it's performing well.

## Disclosure and compliance

- Disclose the affiliate relationship clearly and conspicuously, before the recommendation,
  not buried in fine print — this is a legal requirement (e.g. FTC guidance in the US) in most
  jurisdictions, not just a trust nicety.
- Disclosure language should be plain: "I earn a commission if you buy through this link, at
  no extra cost to you" beats vague or jargon-heavy disclaimers.
- Never imply an independent, unsponsored review when a commission relationship exists —
  it's both a compliance risk and a trust risk if the audience discovers it independently.
- Re-check individual vendor affiliate terms for channel-specific restrictions (e.g. no
  bidding on the brand's own name in paid search) before launching a campaign.

## Multi-brand list management

Promoting several different brands to one list is normal and often the whole business model —
manage it deliberately:

- **Sequence, don't stack.** Don't run two unrelated promotions to the same segment in the
  same week — pick one active promotion at a time per segment and let the nurture-to-offer
  cycle (see `the-fundamentals.md`) complete before starting the next.
- **Segment by interest/purchase history** where possible, so a promotion goes to the
  subscribers most likely to want that specific brand, not the whole list indiscriminately.
- **Keep the value-to-pitch ratio intact per core principle #9** — a list that gets promotion
  after promotion with no non-pitch content in between will churn or tune out fast. The 3:1
  nurture-to-pitch ratio from `playbooks/email-marketing.md` applies here too, arguably more
  strictly, since there's no product loyalty holding the relationship together — only trust in
  you as the curator.
- **Track performance per brand, not just in aggregate** — a brand that converts well but
  generates disproportionate complaints or refund-related replies is a signal to drop it even
  if the earnings look fine, since it's spending down trust you'll need for the next offer.

## Content and copy adjustments

- Comparison and "best of" content (see `playbooks/content-strategy.md`) is a core affiliate
  format — structure it around the reader's decision, not a feature-by-feature product dump:
  who is each option actually for, and which one matches the reader's specific situation.
- Since you can't back the guarantee yourself, be explicit that the guarantee/support is the
  vendor's — don't imply a promise you can't personally stand behind.
- Personal experience/proof (you've used it, a specific result you got) outperforms generic
  praise — see `core-principles.md` #6 (proof outranks assertion) — this is especially true for
  affiliate content, where the audience is primed to expect a sales incentive and looks for
  a personal, credible signal that offsets it.
